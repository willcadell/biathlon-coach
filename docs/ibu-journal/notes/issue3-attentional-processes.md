# Attentional processes in biathlon shooting
Heinrich, Ahrens & Finkenzeller. Journal of Biathlon Coaching, Issue 3, Jan 2025, pp. 24-27. DOI 10.71006/yftc5525. Read in full (4 pages).

Framework: Nideffer's attention model, two axes. Broad vs narrow (many cues vs few) and external vs internal (the environment or the effect of the movement vs one's own body and thoughts). On the course, a broad external focus helps (terrain, rivals); at the mat, narrow and external is what precision needs. Attention has to shift between them on demand.

Evidence summary: research on motor learning favours an external focus (the target, the effect) over an internal one (breath, trigger finger mechanics). The "quiet eye", the last steady fixation on the target before the action, of about 200-300 ms in elite shooters, is linked to better accuracy, though studies in biathlon are mixed (benefit under pressure in one, no link in another). Fast, precise eye jumps between targets also look useful under time pressure.

Practical recommendations (authors, from research and practice):
- Find the target before the rifle comes to the eye (starts the aiming process); find it again at once through the sight; avoid blinking.
- A physical or verbal trigger to start external focus: closing the bolt, a cue word ("go", "now", "black"), breathing out.
- Blink ideally only while bolting; track the targets, not the sights, one at a time.
- Peak target focus should coincide with the trigger pull. Follow through with the eye as well as the hand: watch the hit until the paddle falls before moving on.
- Gaze must be uninterrupted for long enough; after a blink, a lapse or a distraction, hold longer or re-focus.
- Small eye sway or sight movement off target: re-focus. After several misses or a big distraction: take a breath and bring attention back.
- Complex situations (wind, new distractions) need longer focus. On days when focus comes easily, shoot a little faster; on days it does not, it may take longer but can still be clean.
- Cue words can hold concentration across the five shots (for example "centre", "hit", "black").

Applies to live and dry fire. Evidence: mixed research base plus coaching experience; the authors are a sport psychologist, a former national-team coach and a university researcher.
