# Role of core stability in biathlon shooting
Becker & Cobb (Montana State Univ.). Journal of Biathlon Coaching, Issue 1, Jan 2024, pp. 6-9. DOI 10.71006/nbxe9687. Read in full (4 pages).

Why it matters: standing shooting is last in a race with little time to recover, hit rates are lower than prone, and in pursuits standing performance ranks second only to start position in deciding the result. Less body and rifle sway across the line of fire goes with better standing shooting, and older, higher-level athletes sway less. Core stability here means keeping the torso and pelvis in equilibrium when something pushes on them (breathing, fatigue, wind), which is not the same as strength.

Study (30 athletes: youth, junior/senior, masters): a biathlon-specific core stability test (30 s dry-fire hold while seated on an unstable surface on a force plate), then four five-shot dry-fire magazines, half at rest heart rate and half after threshold-intensity running intervals. Worse core stability went with more rifle and body movement in the last half-second before the shot, in both directions of sway, and the link was stronger with elevated heart rate. Youth and masters had worse core stability than junior/senior. Authors' takeaway: core stability may be a key physical quality to build, especially in young athletes, and a buffer against fatigue-related loss of steadiness.

Measuring without a force plate: aim-path length or variability from a Scatt/Noptel in place of centre-of-pressure; or a Y-balance test (found to correlate with core stability in the same study), which needs no kit and also says something about balance on a ski.

Improving it: no evidence-based programme exists yet. Suggested: planks and variants in strength sessions; adapt IBU Exercise Catalogue dry-fire exercises that take the legs out of the picture: seated on an unstable surface (Bosu ball), pulls across and along the line of fire, and pulls on the rifle (catalogue pages 18, 21-22, 56, 58-59). Any exercise that removes reliance on the lower body and makes the torso hold position under disturbance is a candidate; coaches are encouraged to invent their own. Bonus: makes athletes adaptable in difficult conditions.

Evidence: one small lab study (n = 30) plus theory; authors say research here is early.
